Abstract                                MA
                                   D
Due to its presence in many biological systems, the oxidation of dopamine to polydopamine is
of high interest with regard to biomedical and technical applications. Many variations in the
                          PT
reaction mechanisms have been discussed in the scientific literature, indicating a strong

                             E
influence of the particular reaction conditions chosen. In this paper a cyclic voltammetric
study on a gold electrode in phosphate buffered saline at pH 5.8 and 7.0 has been performed
                  CE
to elucidate the anodic oxidation at physiologic pH conditions. Calculation of the pH
dependent species distribution of dopamine was used to identify the relevant species involved
            AC
in the first step of the anodic oxidation. In the pH range 5.8 – 7.0 the phenolic groups of
dopamine are not present in dissociated form and also the amino group is present in
protonated form. The oxidation at the gold electrode is an electrochemically reversible
process and leads to formation of a semiquinone as an intermediate. The follow-up reactions
of the anodically formed intermediates depend on the solution pH. At pH 7.0 the formation of
brown polymeric products on the electrode is faster and compared to experiments at pH 5.8 a
lower cathodic peak current (Ip)c is observed during the reverse scan. The semiquinone
intermediate explains formation of ring-bridged dimers as oligomeric precursors in
polydopamine formation. Even at pH 7 the amino group still is present as ammonium ion,

    1. Introduction




                                                                   PT
The catecholamine dopamine is a hormone essential for our well-being (Figure 1). Dopamine
is a rather reactive molecule that oxidizes easily to polydopamine at physiological conditions.

                                                              RI
Polydopamine is a melanin-like [1], black polymeric aggregate. Polydopamine has been
demonstrated to show protective effects on human cells, at the same time highly reactive

                                                       SC
intermediate species are formed, which provoke severe damage through oxidative stress. Both
effects, of protective and destructive kind, are observed in Parkinson’s Disease [2]. But not

                                                NU
only inside, also outside of our bodies dopamine has gained much interest throughout the last
decades: it was in 1981 when L- DOPA has been discovered as the “glue” for mussels [3] to
attach efficiently to wet surfaces. This discovery has inspired scientists to make use of the
                                        MA
unique molecule dopamine. In material sciences, Lee et al. have developed the first oxidative
polydopamine coating-method [4] in 2007 by auto-oxidation of dopamine in slightly alkaline
medium.

There are four main ways to stimulate polydopamine-formation for coating: by (1) alkaline
auto-oxidation [4,5] or (2) using strong oxidizing agents [6], by (3) UV-triggered oxidation
[7,8] or (4) electrochemically-induced oxidation [9]. In however way polydopamine-
formation is induced, the oxidation pathway of dopamine is a complex multi-step reaction
which involves several steps and reaction intermediates of dopamine. The first oxidation
product of dopamine is dopamine quinone (DQ, see Figure 1), a highly reactive intermediate
and the starting point for polydopamine formation.

It was already in 1967 when the electrochemical behaviour and oxidation mechanism of
catechols [10] have been studied in detail. Soon the “electrode fouling” of catecholamines has
become a well-known phenomenon [11]. A detailed study on electrochemical deposition of
dopamine has shed new light on how the coating-ability of dopamine depends on pH,
dopamine concentration and ionic strength [9]. The use of oxidizing agents like copper [12] or
NaIO4 [6] speeds up the overall reaction and enables coating also in acidic environments. Also
UV-triggered oxidation [7,8] has been employed like a switch to turn polydopamine formation
“on”, while antioxidants like ascorbic acid may turn the reaction “off”.
                                                                  PT
The oxidative formation of polymer layers based on dopamine are of high interest for
                                                             RI
antibacterial [13,14] and antifouling coatings [15,16]. In solutions of pH = 7 and higher, the
oxidation of dopamine to the brown-black coloured polydopamine proceeds spontaneously
                                                        SC
through action of the dissolved oxygen. The spontaneous oxidation of dopamine can be used
to coat a metal surface e.g. by dipping the substrate into a solution containing dopamine at pH

                                                NU
8.5 or higher. The polydopamine-layer will form through oxidation of dopamine by O2, which
however is a time-consuming procedure and hard to control.

                                        MA
For the formation of polydopamine layers as surface coatings on metal surfaces anodic
oxidation of dopamine could be a preferable approach to concentrate the oxidative reaction
near to the surface of the metal anode. Coating via electrochemically-induced oxidation
                                   D
allows for uniform coatings in short times, just a few seconds - with a minimal waste of

                         PT
material. As dopamine is with 3.2 USD per gram a rather expensive chemical, this approach is
of high relevance for larger scale applications [17].
                            E
Li and co-workers [9] investigated the electrochemical deposition of dopamine by use of a

                  CE
quartz crystal microbalance to study the polydopamine growth on Au surfaces.
They investigated dopamine in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) with different dopamine

            AC
concentrations at distinct pH values between 2 and 11. For this setup, an ECE mechanism has
been proposed, which at least involved transfer of 4 electrons and release of 4 H+, 2 each per
E step. Another earlier study on electrochemical oxidation of dopamine at physiological pH
proposes an ECC in a sequence of transfer of 2 electrons and release of 2 protons (E),
ammonium deprotonation (C) and Michael-type ring closure (C) [18].
In the cyclic voltammetry of quinones the mechanism of the cathodic reduction of a quinone
is separated into a series of EC reactions which involve 1 e- and 1 H+ [19]. In analogy in the
anodic oxidation of dopamine, radicals could be involved as intermediates. However this very
first step has not been considered in sufficient depth for the anodic oxidation mechanism of
dopamine.

In this study, the critical pH-window between pH 5 and 7, where auto-oxidation begins to
occur, has been chosen for a detailed study of the anodic oxidation of dopamine on solid-state
electrodes. Calculations of the pH-dependent species distribution of dopamine were used to
identify the major species involved in the initial anodic oxidation. Cyclic voltammetry
experiments at different scan rates were used to elucidate the mechanism of the anodic
dopamine oxidation in detail and to investigate for a possible radical-based reaction
mechanism.

Corona-Avendano et al. investigated 13C-NMR shifts [25] for dopamine at pH 5.1 vs. pH 9.6.
At pH 5.1 the fully protonated species I-DPAH3+ predominates, while at pH 9.6 the second

Addressing the second question, which of the phenolic groups will be deprotonated
preferentially as first step, it is very likely that the negative charge arising after the first
                                                                       PT
